Securities Issues in Private Credit

The fastest-growing corner of private capital

Dallas Bar Association Securities Section · August 24, 2026 · Jason T. Barnes
The frame
Three securities analyses stacked on one loan.

The vehicle, the instrument and the distribution are independent questions. Clearing one does not clear the others. Run all three or you have not run the analysis.

01Three levels, three different questions

1The vehicle

Whoever raises the capital is almost certainly selling a security: fund interest, note program or JV unit. The 1933 Act, the Investment Company Act and the Advisers Act engage here.

2The instrument

The loan is usually not a security. That is a conclusion, not an assumption. Reves governs, and the answer moves with the plan of distribution.

3The distribution

Anyone paid for locating capital, borrowers or investors may be a broker under Exchange Act §15(a)(1), whatever the arrangement is called.

02Is the loan a security? The Reves family resemblance test

Every note is presumed to be a security. The presumption is rebutted only if the note strongly resembles a judicially recognized exception, tested against four factors.

FactorPoints toward a securityPoints away
Motivations of buyer and sellerInvestment motive; raising capitalCommercial or corporate purpose
Plan of distributionBroad offering; free transferabilityConsent-based assignment; minimum assignment amounts; no assignment to natural persons
Reasonable expectations of the publicMarketing that leans on “investor,” “investment” and “yield”Documents and marketing that describe a loan
Another regulatory scheme reducing riskNo other oversight; unsecuredBank regulatory guidance; perfected collateral; first-priority lien

Kirschner v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, 79 F.4th 290 (2d Cir. 2023), cert. denied (2024)

Notes under a $1.775 billion term loan syndicated to some 400 institutional investors were held not to be securities. The plan of distribution weighed heavily: consent requirements, high minimum assignments and a bar on assignment to natural persons. Not nationally binding, and every factor the court relied on was a drafting choice.

Run the contrast. An institutional club loan with consent-based assignment sits comfortably outside Reves. A CRE bridge note fractionalized into thirty passive interests, marketed as yield, with no transfer restrictions, is a different case on three of four factors.

03Fund-level architecture: the four statutes

The offering: Securities Act of 1933

  • Rule 506(b) with pre-existing relationships; 506(c) with verification to market openly.
  • Form D within 15 days of first sale; Texas notice filing.
  • Section 17(a) and Rule 10b-5 apply to exempt offerings.

The manager: Investment Advisers Act

  • §203(m) private fund adviser exemption below $150 million; §203(l) for venture.
  • Fiduciary duty and the marketing rule apply whether or not the adviser is registered.

The vehicle: Investment Company Act

  • §3(c)(1) for 100 or fewer beneficial owners; §3(c)(7) for qualified purchasers.
  • For real estate credit, §3(c)(5)(C) is the workhorse. It is an asset-mix test: preferred equity and mezzanine positions must be tested, not assumed.

The distribution: Exchange Act

  • §15(a) broker registration for anyone paid to locate capital or investors.
  • §29(b) rescission exposure where an unregistered broker was compensated.

05When debt carries equity

The loan and the kicker are two offerings, analyzed separately. The kicker needs its own exemption, investor representations, disclosure and Form D coverage.

  • To whom is it issued? One institutional mezzanine fund is simple. Preferred sold to eleven individuals introduced by a mortgage broker is a private placement with a Form D, a Texas filing and a broker question.
  • Does the borrower have room? Preemptive rights, investor consents, operating agreement transfer limits and senior lender equity-transfer bans. A closing condition, not cleanup.

06Moving the paper and getting paid for it

  • Assignment: consent, minimum amounts and no natural persons are the Reves-protective features.
  • Participation: historically outside the securities laws, but not automatically.
  • Fractionalized interests sold to passive individuals with no consent rights: the structure that fails Reves.
  • Transaction-based pay for locating capital is the strongest indicator of broker status. The 2023 M&A broker exemption (§15(b)(13)) does not reach credit fund raises or note offerings. Texas registration applies independently.

07Representing the capital source

  • Paper the loan so Reves comes out as intended; keep “investor,” “investment” and “broker” out where a neutral word will do.
  • Give the warrant or preferred its own exemption analysis and filing.
  • Diligence how the fund’s own capital was raised; confirm no unregistered transaction-based pay.
  • Real estate: test the 3(c)(5)(C) mix continuously; fix valuation and appraisal methodology before the first mark.

08Representing the borrower or sponsor

  • Read the equity kicker as a securities issuance by your client.
  • Preferred equity is not a loan, whatever the term sheet calls it.
  • Gap capital from the sponsor’s own investor list is the sponsor’s own offering.
  • No finder success fee out of loan proceeds until broker status is resolved.
  • Ask the client to name every person who will receive money at closing, and why.
