Securities Compliance in M&A

A deal lawyer’s playbook: securities issues, rollover structures and seller wealth protection

Dallas Bar Association Securities Section · February 2026 · Jason T. Barnes
Key question
Is any form of consideration a security?

Rollover equity, seller notes and earnouts are routinely treated as non-securities. Economic substance controls, not the label the parties chose.

01The regulatory framework in M&A

Federal securities laws

The 1933 Act governs offers and sales of securities, even in private deals. The 1934 Act adds antifraud, reporting and broker rules.

State blue sky laws

State registration and exemption requirements overlay federal exemptions. Often overlooked under deal-timeline pressure.

Exemptions and safe harbors

Section 4(a)(2) and Rule 506(b) or (c) are the workhorses. Section 3(a)(9) exchanges have strict conditions.

02Characterizing the seller’s consideration

InstrumentThe testWhat to do at issuance
Rollover equityHowey: investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profits from others’ efforts. LLC and LP interests almost always qualify.Identify and document the exemption when issued. A securities memo or opinion should accompany any rollover, whatever the deal size.
Seller notesReves: every note is presumed a security unless it resembles a recognized non-security, judged on purpose, distribution, expectations and other regulation.Run the analysis; do not treat the note purely as a loan instrument.
EarnoutsContingent consideration tied to future performance can be a security depending on structure.Analyze for exemption compliance at issuance, not after a dispute arises.

03Disclosure and antifraud

  • Rule 10b-5 reaches material misstatements and omissions in any securities transaction, registered or not.
  • The disclosure record includes management presentations, CIMs, data room materials and seller representations.
  • Projections need cautionary language; separate business judgment from factual representation.
  • Securities counsel should review investor-facing materials before they go out.

04Deal-level issues to spot early

  • Broker status: advisers paid transaction-based compensation may need registration. The 2023 M&A broker exemption (§15(b)(13)) is narrow and fact-specific.
  • Accredited investor status: Rule 506(c) requires verification; under 506(b), do not rely on stale questionnaires at closing.
  • Mischaracterized instruments undermine the exemption analysis and can surface years after closing.

05Structuring the rollover: three lenses at once

Equity or debt

Drives tax, securities and bankruptcy treatment. Thin capitalization, high stated interest and no amortization suggest equity. Hybrids need both analyses.

Tax

Section 721 (partnerships) or 351 (corporations) deferral; conditions must be met at closing. Section 707 disguised sale rules; boot is taxable when received.

Governance

Observer versus board seat. Approval rights over new debt, acquisitions and changes in the business. Budget and key-person provisions for continuing sellers.

07Earnouts: economics, tax and securities

  • Expect less than the maximum. SRS Acquiom: earnouts in 24% of 2025 private-target deals outside life sciences; some payout in about 59% of earnout deals; about 21% of aggregate maximum earnout value paid.
  • EBITDA earnouts are exposed to post-closing accounting elections, overhead allocation and intercompany charges.
  • Negotiate before signing: earnout-specific accounting covenants, change-of-control acceleration, binding dispute resolution.
  • Tax: capital gain versus ordinary income characterization; installment treatment under §453 defers gain but needs upfront structuring.

08Insurance coverage gaps

  • R&W insurance generally excludes known breaches, scheduled matters, purchase price adjustments and forward-looking statements. It often does not cover securities claims based on pre-closing disclosure.
  • D&O tail must be negotiated before closing; six years is common and often cut to save cost.
  • Indemnification in governing documents should survive for a defined period and be funded.
  • Key-person coverage on continuing sellers is routinely left unaddressed.

09After closing

  • The regulatory tail: securities claims carry limitation periods of one to five years depending on the claim; some run from discovery.
  • Representations given by former owners can create personal exposure if they prove false, even without fraudulent intent.
  • Public buyer stock: affiliates face Rule 144 volume limits and holding periods; lock-ups restrict selling and hedging.

Three questions every seller should answer at closing

  1. What exactly do I hold?
  2. When can I sell or transfer it?
  3. Who is responsible for telling me when something changes?

Keep securities counsel, a tax advisor and a wealth manager engaged at closing, with a post-closing compliance calendar prepared in advance.
