A continuation vehicle lets a private equity sponsor keep an asset it believes in after the fund that owns it reaches the end of its life. The existing fund sells the asset to a new fund managed by the same sponsor. Investors in the old fund choose between taking cash at the deal price or rolling their interest into the new vehicle, and new investors, usually led by a dedicated secondaries fund, supply the cash for those who sell.

The structure has moved from niche to mainstream. By the Institutional Limited Partners Association’s count, continuation vehicles accounted for roughly 14 percent of sponsor-backed exits globally in 2025, up from 5 percent in 2021.

The reason these transactions draw scrutiny is simple to state. The sponsor sits on both sides of the price. It manages the selling fund and owes that fund a fiduciary duty to get the best available price. It also manages the buying vehicle, will earn fees from it, typically rolls its own carried interest into it, and benefits if the price leaves the new vehicle room to perform. Every feature of a well-run process exists to manage that one fact.

The rule is gone. The duty is not.

In 2023 the SEC adopted a package of private fund adviser rules that, among other things, would have required a registered adviser to obtain a fairness or valuation opinion before completing an adviser-led secondary such as a continuation vehicle. On June 5, 2024, the Fifth Circuit vacated the entire package in National Association of Private Fund Managers v. SEC.

Reading that decision as a lower bar would be a mistake. Four sources of obligation remain in place:

  • The Investment Advisers Act. Section 206 imposes a fiduciary duty of care and loyalty on every investment adviser, registered or not. Conflicts must be fully and fairly disclosed, and consent to them must be informed.
  • The pooled vehicle antifraud rule. Rule 206(4)-8 prohibits misleading statements to investors and prospective investors in a fund, not only to the fund itself.
  • The fund agreement. The limited partnership agreement sets the conflict provisions and the mechanics of advisory committee consent.
  • Delaware law. A Delaware partnership agreement can shape or limit fiduciary duties, but it cannot eliminate the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

The practical effect is that process now does the work a regulation would have done. The vacatur removed a checklist. It did not remove the obligation the checklist was written to satisfy.

What institutional investors now expect

On October 20, 2026, ILPA published its final continuation vehicle guidance, Continuation Vehicles: Advancing Alignment Through Best Practices, which replaces its 2023 guidance. ILPA guidance is not law, and the final version describes itself as designed to guide rather than dictate. It is, however, the yardstick an advisory committee, an arbitrator, and an SEC examiner are all likely to reach for. That makes any departure from it something to explain in writing before the deal, not after.

Two points in the final guidance stand out. It recommends that investors generally have no fewer than 20 clear business days after receiving the final election package to decide whether to sell or roll, with more time for complex or multi-asset transactions. And it frames the economic test as whether rolling investors, on the whole, are left no worse off than if the continuation vehicle had not happened.

Read together with ILPA’s January 2026 disclosure template, the guidance points to six elements of a process that will hold up:

  1. An evidenced rationale. Why a continuation vehicle rather than a sale to a third party, an IPO, a partial sale, or a distribution in kind, with the alternatives actually examined.
  2. Real price discovery. A competitive process that an outsider would recognize as competitive, a fairness opinion, and an anonymized summary of the final bids.
  3. An informed advisory committee. Early engagement, independent advisers paid by the fund, and time to meet without the sponsor in the room.
  4. Time and information parity. One data room and one account of the asset and its exit prospects for existing investors and new buyers alike, with the election clock starting only when the package is complete.
  5. Real choices. Sell, roll, or a combination. An option to remain in place on existing fee and carry terms is the benchmark; if it is not offered, say why.
  6. Sponsor alignment. The sponsor rolls its carried interest unless there is a stated reason not to, discloses any de-risking, and allocates transaction costs to the parties who benefit.

A case study in process

In December 2025 the Abu Dhabi Investment Council sued affiliates of the Energy & Minerals Group in the Delaware Court of Chancery over a proposed sale of a stake in Ascent Resources from one EMG fund to an EMG continuation fund. The case is instructive because, by published accounts, the sponsor did what a checklist would require. It engaged a financial adviser to run a competitive process, sought advisory committee approval, obtained a fairness opinion, and offered existing investors both a cash-out and a rollover option.

The complaint attacks the process around those steps. It alleges inadequate notice and information to advisory committee members before the vote, no response to requests to delay the vote and meet without the sponsor present, different information for committee members than for prospective buyers, and no process for alternatives such as a public offering despite recent discussions of one. The parties agreed to pause the transaction so the dispute could proceed in arbitration, as the fund documents required.

These are allegations, not findings. Each one, though, describes a process failure that could have been seen in real time: compressed time, inconsistent accounts to different audiences, and an alternative that was never tested.

Questions worth asking

For sponsors and their counsel:

  • Have we documented why a continuation vehicle is the right path, including the alternatives we rejected and why?
  • Would an outside observer call our price discovery competitive?
  • Are the advisory committee, existing investors, and new buyers seeing the same information and the same account of exit plans?
  • Does the committee have its own advisers, enough time, and a session without us?
  • Who are counsel’s clients? If one firm represents the sponsor, the selling fund, and the new vehicle, it represents a seller and a buyer in a related-party sale, and the consents it needs are its own clients’, not only the committee’s.

For investors and advisory committee members:

  • Ask for the completed ILPA disclosure template, the valuation report, and the terms on which the valuation firm was engaged.
  • Insist that the election period run from a complete package.
  • Check whether your side letter rights carry over to the new vehicle.
  • Treat information received as a committee member as restricted.
  • Ask the organizing question directly: who is on both sides of this price, and what has been done about it?

The same question, beyond continuation vehicles

“Who is on both sides of the price?” travels well. When a semi-liquid fund makes a repurchase offer, the fund calculates the net asset value and buys at it. When a private company runs a tender offer for employee shares, the company knows more about its own prospects than any employee selling to it. An April 2026 SEC exemptive order shortened the minimum offering period for qualifying private company self-tenders from 20 business days to 10, which makes these programs easier to run. It did not touch the antifraud rules, which is why the offer document remains the selling employees’ main protection.

In each setting, the answer to that one question tells you where the conflict sits, what disclosure is owed, and whether someone at the table needs a lawyer of their own.
