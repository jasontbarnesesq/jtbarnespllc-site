Is Your Loan a Security? Three Questions for Private Credit Lenders and Borrowers

A private credit deal is a lending transaction and, very often, three separate securities questions: the fund that raised the money, the loan itself, and whoever was paid to find the capital. Where each one bites, for corporate and real estate credit alike.

All insightsArticlesSeriesSpeaking & CLETopicsNewsletter

Private credit is lending by someone other than a bank: a private fund, an insurance company, a family office, or a club of investors. Over the past decade it has taken over a large share of the lending that banks used to do, from senior loans to sponsor-backed companies to bridge and mezzanine financing for commercial real estate. Estimates of the market’s size vary widely, and the reason is worth noticing. There is no agreed definition of what counts, which is also why the regulatory boundary around it is contested.

Most participants think of a private credit deal as a lending transaction. It is one. It is also, very often, three separate securities law questions stacked on top of each other, and each one has to be answered on its own.

Three questions, not one

  1. The vehicle. Whoever raised the money being lent was almost certainly selling a security, whether it was called a fund interest, a note program, or a joint venture unit. That brings in the Securities Act, the Investment Company Act, and the Investment Advisers Act.
  2. The instrument. The loan itself is usually not a security. That is a conclusion to be reached, not an assumption, and the answer can change with how the loan is sold on and to whom.
  3. The distribution. Anyone paid for finding capital, borrowers, or investors may be acting as a securities broker, regardless of what the engagement letter or the business card says.

These questions are independent. A transaction can clear the instrument question cleanly and fail the distribution question badly. In practice, the exposure in most deals is not that one question was answered wrong. It is that only one or two of them were asked.

Is the loan a security?

The Supreme Court’s test for whether a promissory note is a security comes from Reves v. Ernst & Young (1990). Every note is presumed to be a security. The presumption gives way only if the note strongly resembles a type of debt that courts have already recognized as something else, such as an ordinary commercial loan. Courts weigh four factors:

  • Motivations. If the buyer is investing for a return and the seller is raising capital, that points toward a security. A commercial or corporate purpose points away.
  • Plan of distribution. Broad offering to the public points toward a security. Transfer restrictions, minimum assignment amounts, and a bar on selling to individuals point away.
  • Reasonable expectations of the public. What the documents and the marketing call the instrument matters. Materials that lean on “investor,” “investment,” and “yield” cut the wrong way.
  • Another regulatory scheme. Bank regulatory oversight, perfected collateral, and a first-priority lien reduce the need for securities law protection.

The leading modern application is Kirschner v. JPMorgan Chase Bank. In 2023 the Second Circuit held that notes issued under a roughly $1.775 billion term loan, syndicated to some 400 institutional investors, were not securities. Among the features the court weighed in the plan of distribution: assignments required consent, minimum assignment amounts were high, and assignment to individuals was prohibited. The Supreme Court declined to review the case in February 2024.

Two cautions follow. The decision binds only in the Second Circuit. And every feature the court relied on was a drafting choice. Contrast an institutional club loan with consent-based assignment against a commercial real estate bridge note divided into thirty interests, sold to passive individual investors, marketed as a yield product, with no transfer restrictions. Same asset class, opposite answer on three of the four factors, and the difference lies in documents the lawyers wrote.

The equity kicker is a separate offering

Private credit frequently carries some upside: warrants issued alongside the loan, a conversion right, preferred equity with a back-end participation, or an exit fee tied to the value of the property. The loan and the equity piece are two different instruments. The loan may sit entirely outside the securities laws while the warrant or the preferred unit plainly does not. The equity piece needs its own exemption, its own investor representations, its own disclosure, and its own Form D coverage.

Two questions are worth asking every time. First, who actually receives the equity? A warrant issued to one institutional mezzanine fund is straightforward. A preferred equity piece sold to eleven individuals introduced by the sponsor’s mortgage broker is a private placement with a Form D filing, a Texas notice filing, and a broker question attached. Second, does the borrower have room to issue it? Preemptive rights, existing investor consents, transfer restrictions in the operating agreement and, in real estate, the senior lender’s limits on equity transfers all bear on whether the kicker can be delivered at all. That is a closing condition, not a cleanup item.

Who is getting paid to find the money?

Being paid a percentage for bringing in capital, borrowers, or investors is the strongest single indicator that someone is acting as a broker under Section 15(a) of the Securities Exchange Act. Common examples in private credit include capital raisers paid a share of a fund’s raise, finders paid a share of the fee for introducing a borrower to a lender, and mortgage brokers who move from placing the loan to placing investors in the loan or in the preferred equity above it.

The federal M&A broker exemption that took effect in March 2023 does not solve this. It covers brokers who facilitate the sale of control of smaller private companies. It does not reach capital raising for credit funds or note offerings. Texas dealer and agent registration also applies on its own terms, separately from the federal analysis.

The consequence that deserves attention is in Section 29(b) of the Exchange Act, which makes a contract made in violation of the Act voidable at the election of the innocent party. That exposure runs against the transaction, not only against the unregistered finder. It belongs in diligence, not in the fee letter.

Corporate and real estate credit fail in different places

Corporate credit deals tend to run into trouble on the equity kicker and on the person who found the money. Real estate credit deals tend to run into trouble on passive investor capital, on preferred equity that is described as debt, and on whether the lending fund still fits its own Investment Company Act exemption. Real estate debt funds commonly rely on Section 3(c)(5)(C), which turns on the mix of mortgages and other real estate interests the fund holds. Preferred equity and mezzanine positions move that ratio, so the test has to be run continuously, not only at formation.

Practical steps

For lenders and fund managers:

  • Paper the loan so the Reves analysis comes out the way you intend: consent-based assignment, minimum assignment amounts, no assignment to individuals, and neutral vocabulary in the documents and the marketing.
  • Give any warrant or preferred equity its own exemption analysis and filing.
  • Confirm that no one in the capital chain received transaction-based pay without registration.
  • For real estate credit, test the fund’s Investment Company Act exemption every time the portfolio mix changes.

For borrowers and sponsors:

  • Treat an equity kicker as a securities issuance by your company, and confirm you have room to issue it.
  • If gap capital is coming from your own investor list, that is your own securities offering, with its own exemption, disclosure, and Texas filing.
  • Do not let a finder be paid a success fee out of loan proceeds until broker status is resolved.
  • Keep a record of what was told to the lender. Antifraud exposure travels with the equity component even where the loan is not a security.

The most useful single question, on either side, is this: name every person who will receive money at closing, and why. Anyone paid for finding capital belongs in the securities analysis, not only in the sources and uses. And because the cheap fixes all live in the term sheet, the time to ask is before the documents circulate.
